# Research: adult coaching language for 4-6yo tabletop games

## Q1 — Question-based correction instead of "wrong"

VERIFIED. Two distinct named techniques in L2/language-teaching lit, applied to early-childhood language work too:
- **Recast**: adult repeats child's error back in corrected form, no explicit "wrong" flag. Implicit, doesn't break communication flow. In classroom feedback studies recast is *most used* but *least effective* for uptake — led to no uptake 69% of time (student rarely generates the fix themselves). [Recast (language teaching) - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recast_(language_teaching))
- **Elicitation**: adult restates up to the point of error and stops, prompting child to self-correct/fill gap — a real question-based technique. Same corpus: elicitation led to uptake 100% of time vs recast's 31%. [ELL Strategies - TanKHuynh](https://tankhuynh.com/oral-errors/), [Types of Corrective Feedback - CARLA](https://archive.carla.umn.edu/cobaltt/modules/strategies/c_feedback.pdf)
- Early-childhood-specific: shared book-reading study w/ pre-K found teachers **underuse** recast/elicit scaffolds despite evidence they help syntax skill; not disproven for young age, just under-applied. [ASHA/LSHSS 2021 shared book reading study](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_LSHSS-21-00020)

**Wait time**: systematic review found 4 studies experimentally manipulated wait time w/ children (brief 1s vs extended 3-10s), all found extended wait time positive. One preschool-specific study (Lee et al. 1987) compared 1s vs 5s wait — benefit was significant for atypically-developing children, **no significant difference for typically-developing preschoolers**. So documented "the number" for wait time = commonly 3-10s range in general question-wait-time lit, but preschool-specific evidence for self-correction is thin/mixed. [Wait Time Systematic Review - PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11545128/)

DESIGN OPINION: no single named "ask-a-question-not-say-wrong" preschool technique found bundled as one term — elicitation is the closest real term but literature base is mostly L2/language, not board-game rule-correction. Applying to game-rule mistakes (not speech errors) is an extrapolation, not directly evidenced.

## Q2 — Instruction length / steps

VERIFIED, general consensus, no single canonical study:
- 3-yo: ~2-step directions. 4-yo: ~3-step. 5-yo: 3-5 step directions "in correct order" typical, with support (visual cues, acting out) pushing toward 5. [Empowered Parents](https://empoweredparents.co/2-step-directions-for-preschoolers/), [PMC 5-yo working memory transparent goal cue](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6814763/)
- Working-memory research: 5-yos show improved multi-step performance specifically when task embeds a **transparent goal cue** (visual/concrete anchor) — 4-yos didn't benefit from same cue. Implication: pairing instruction with a visible referent (e.g., pointing at board) measurably helps 5yo, not 4yo.
- Sentence-length guidance (Hanen Centre, SLT-oriented): keep sentences **short but grammatically well-formed** — don't over-simplify to point of bad grammar, kids understand more than they produce. No hard word-count figure found (no "X words max" number is documented) — closest concrete practice: "break request into short chunks/steps," "emphasize key words" via stress/gesture. [Hanen Centre](https://www.hanen.org/information-tips/how-to-help-your-child-use-early-sentences), [ISBE ECERS-3 language guidance](https://www.isbe.net/Documents/ECERS-3_Language_Samples_Teachers.pdf)

DESIGN OPINION: "3 steps max" is a reasonable practice synthesis, not a single cited hard limit.

## Q3 — Process/effort praise vs generic praise — CONTESTED, be honest

VERIFIED core study: **Kamins & Dweck 1999**, Developmental Psychology — 5-6yo children role-played tasks; person-praise/criticism produced significantly more "helpless" responses (giving up, negative self-eval) after setback than process-praise/criticism. This is the actual preschool-age study (not Mueller & Dweck 1998, which was 5th graders). [Kamins & Dweck 1999 PDF](https://people.uncw.edu/hungerforda/infancy/pdf/person%20versus%20process%20praise%20and%20criticism.pdf)

CONTESTED / replication-weak at the broader "growth mindset" level:
- Mueller & Dweck 1998 (5th graders, not preschool) failed to replicate in Li & Bates 2019; effect size collapsed. Dweck's camp disputes replication's power/design (claims post-failure question wasn't testable because Li&Bates kids scored ~2.5 SD higher, i.e. no real failure trial occurred).
- Vocational-ed replication (Glerum et al.) found **zero effect** of praise type.
- Meta-analysis (Sisk et al. 2018) on mindset interventions broadly: d=.08, "trivial."
- Conflict-of-interest note: most defending papers co-authored by Dweck herself.

**Honest read**: the specific 1999 preschool finding (person vs process praise → helplessness) is fairly robust/frequently cited and hasn't itself been the main replication target. It's the *broader* "growth mindset" program (mostly older-kids studies) that's shaky. Recommend citing Kamins & Dweck 1999 for the preschool claim, flag that broader Dweck mindset literature is contested. [Scientific American debate summary](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/debate-arises-over-teaching-growth-mindsets-to-motivate-students/), [progressfocused.com rebuttal](https://www.progressfocused.com/2020/09/growth-mindset-debunked-not-so-much.html)

No documented example phrasing found beyond category labels (process praise = "you worked hard / tried a good strategy" style vs person praise = "you're so smart/good" style, as used in the coded transcripts) — original papers don't give a reusable script bank.

## Q4 — Ending activity while child still wants more

VERIFIED practice guidance (not experimental research, this is early-childhood-ed practitioner consensus):
- Advance warning is standard: layered warnings recommended — e.g. Head Start: "You have five more minutes to play, finish up" → "two minutes left, we're about to clean up." Some sources use 10/5/1-min three-stage warnings. [HeadStart.gov transitions](https://headstart.gov/transitions/article/support-transitions-infants-toddlers), [Teaching Foundations LLC](https://www.teachingfoundationsllc.com/classroommanagement/how-to-design-transitions-to-prevent-challenging-behaviors)
- Sensory/non-verbal cues paired w/ verbal: timers (visual, since kids lack time-sense), light flicker, sound cues (e.g. triangle strikes).
- Caution documented: over-warning trains kids to ignore early warnings and wait for the last one — consistency/predictability matters more than warning count.
- "Leave them wanting more" framed as: transitions are hardest specifically when child is deeply engaged in a *preferred* activity — analogized to "end of the best concert of your life." This is the practice rationale for stopping before satiation, not a controlled study result.

DESIGN OPINION: "stop while they still want to play" itself is a well-known parenting/coaching heuristic (also common in dog-training-style / motivation psych framing) but I found no controlled preschool study directly testing that exact stopping-point rule against a "play to natural end" control — it's inferred practice wisdom, not an RCT finding.

## Q5 — Observable disengagement signals in 4-6yo, adult-led tabletop tasks

IMPORTANT CONTESTED FINDING — gaze aversion is NOT reliably a disengagement signal:
- Doherty-Sneddon et al. line of research: gaze aversion during a hard question is a **positive** sign of active cognitive processing/concentration, not disengagement — kids look away to reduce cognitive load while thinking. Teachers interrupting a gaze-averted child may disrupt useful thinking. [Helping children think: Gaze aversion and teaching - Wiley](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1348/026151005X49872), [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2008/06/080602103336.htm)
- Critically, age-dependent: study of 5-yo vs 8-yo found **older kids** (8yo) show adult-like "look away when question is hard" pattern; **5-yo did not show this consistently** — meaning for the target age band (4-6), gaze aversion is an even *less* reliable signal (neither clearly concentration nor clearly disengagement — just noisy).

Fidgeting: general classroom-observation research (BOSS system, not preschool/tabletop specific) codes off-task motor behavior (touching objects, out-of-seat, head down) as one of 3 off-task categories, but also flags nuance — mild fidgeting can *support* cognitive load management, not just signal disengagement. Distinction drawn: quiet fidget = possibly fine; loud/roaming/disruptive = erodes focus. [Pearson "distraction dilemma" blog](https://www.pearson.com/international-schools/international-schools-blog/2025/12/the-distraction-dilemma--how-your-students-still-learn-with-sele.html)

Materials-manipulation signal (closest to tabletop/board game context, but from general early-childhood materials-engagement lit, not game-specific): disengagement patterns include finishing/abandoning quickly and moving to something else, or abandoning after repeated failed attempts w/ frustration. Positive engagement = persistence, adding complexity, inviting others in. [Kaplan Co blog on classroom materials](https://blog.kaplanco.com/why-children-stop-engaging-with-classroom-materials)

**No documented duration/threshold found** (e.g., "after N seconds of off-task touching = disengaged") for 4-6yo in tabletop/board-game contexts specifically — none of the sources located give a numeric cutoff. Say this plainly: this appears to be an evidence gap; existing literature uses interval-coding (e.g. 15-sec partial-interval recording) as a *research methodology*, not a *practice threshold* a parent-coach could apply.

Attention-span adjacent figure (not disengagement-specific but relevant context for pacing): popular heuristic "2-3 (up to 5) minutes per year of age" → 5yo ~10-15min sustained attention on engaged task. This is a popularized heuristic cited across parenting sites, not a single peer-reviewed formula — flagged as such by the sources themselves. [Cobebe attention span chart](https://cobebe.co.uk/attention-span-by-age/)

## Unresolved / gaps
- No single named technique = "ask a question instead of saying wrong" for preschool *game-rule* correction (as opposed to speech-error correction) — elicitation is closest, evidence base is L2/language acquisition, extrapolation to rule-teaching is design judgment not direct evidence.
- No hard word-count/sentence-length number for preschool instructions; "keep it short + grammatical" is the extent of documented guidance.
- No RCT directly tests "stop early while child still wants more" vs "let activity end naturally" for 4-6yo engagement/motivation outcomes — practice consensus only.
- No numeric duration/threshold for disengagement signals in tabletop context for 4-6yo — flagged as evidence gap, not just omission.
- Gaze aversion guidance is the single most important correction to intuition here: for a parent-coach script, coach language should NOT treat a child looking away as "not listening" — literature (weakly, for older kids more than 5yo) suggests opposite could be true.
